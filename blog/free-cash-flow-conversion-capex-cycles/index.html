CFO to EBITDA and Free Cash Flow Conversion: Auditing Capital Allocation in Indian Equities

How institutional investors audit cash flow conversion in Indian equities: CFO to EBITDA ratios, maintenance vs growth capex, and cash conversion cycles.

Short answer

Auditing cash flow conversion requires comparing cumulative Cash Flow from Operations (CFO) to reported EBITDA and Net Profit over a 5-year business cycle, verifying whether CFO/EBITDA exceeds 70%, and subtracting maintenance capex to evaluate true Free Cash Flow (FCF) available for dividend distributions, debt reduction, or high-ROCE reinvestment.

Key takeaways

  • Reported accounting profit is an opinion, but operating cash flow deposited in the bank is a factual economic reality.
  • A cumulative 5-year CFO-to-EBITDA conversion ratio below 65% signals persistent working capital leakage or aggressive accrual accounting.
  • Separating maintenance capex from growth capex is vital to measuring owner earnings and true enterprise earning power.
  • High-quality compounders consistently generate positive Free Cash Flow even while funding internal capacity expansions.

In equity investing, accounting profit is an opinion, but cash in the bank is a fact. A company can report rising revenue and expanding operating margins while simultaneously bleeding cash into uncollected trade receivables, unsold inventory, and continuous capital expenditures.

The ultimate measure of business quality is its Free Cash Flow (FCF) Conversion: the percentage of accounting profit that actually emerges as liquid cash available to shareholders after funding all necessary capital reinvestment.

Understanding the mechanics of cash conversion separates true compounding engines from capital-destroying optical growth stories.

Auditing the CFO-to-EBITDA Conversion Ratio

The baseline institutional health check is the CFO-to-EBITDA ratio:

$$\text{CFO Conversion Ratio} = \frac{\text{Cumulative 5-Year CFO}}{\text{Cumulative 5-Year EBITDA}}$$

When analyzing this ratio across Indian sectors, analysts look for key warning signs: - Persistent Ratios Below 60%: If a manufacturing firm generates ₹1,000 crore of EBITDA over five years but produces only ₹450 crore of CFO, ₹550 crore of reported profits were consumed by working capital bloat or capitalized as project costs. - Inventory Days Expansion: In consumer durables or specialty chemicals, expanding inventory days during falling raw material prices indicates obsolete stock valuation. - Customer Advances Decay: A drop in customer advances combined with extended payment terms indicates weakening bargaining power against enterprise clients.

Disentangling Maintenance Capex from Growth Capex

To compute true Free Cash Flow, an analyst must subtract capital expenditures from Cash Flow from Operations:

$$\text{Free Cash Flow} = \text{CFO} - \text{Capital Expenditures}$$

However, not all capex is created equal: 1. Maintenance Capex: The capital required to replace aging machinery, repair software infrastructure, and maintain the company’s competitive position. Maintenance capex does not generate incremental revenue; it merely prevents market share loss. 2. Growth Capex: Discretionary capital invested to expand production capacity, launch new product lines, or enter new geographic markets. Growth capex should generate high incremental Return on Capital Employed (ROIC).

If a company cannot fund its maintenance capex out of operating cash flows, it is technically liquidating its capital base from within.

The Reinvestment Rate and Compounding Velocity

True compounders achieve both high cash conversion and high return on incremental capital:

$$\text{Earnings Growth} = \text{Reinvestment Rate} \times \text{ROIC}$$

Where the Reinvestment Rate is defined as:

$$\text{Reinvestment Rate} = \frac{\text{Net Capex} + \Delta \text{Working Capital}}{\text{NOPAT}}$$

A business generating a 25% ROIC that can reinvest 60% of its free cash flow back into the business will compound intrinsic value at $15\%$ per annum without taking on a single rupee of external bank debt.

Frequently asked questions

Why is a 5-year cumulative CFO-to-EBITDA ratio better than a single-year snapshot?

In any single financial year, operating cash flows can fluctuate due to inventory restocking, bulk raw material purchases, or delayed customer collections at year-end. A cumulative 5-year view smooths out cyclical working capital swings and reveals whether accounting profits reliably convert into hard cash over a full business cycle.

How do research analysts distinguish maintenance capex from growth capex in Indian filings?

Indian annual reports do not explicitly segregate maintenance capex from growth capex. Analysts estimate maintenance capex by examining historical annual depreciation expenses, asset turnover trends, and management commentary in the Management Discussion and Analysis (MD&A) regarding baseline plant upkeep versus new greenfield capacity additions.

What happens when a company with high EBITDA generates zero Free Cash Flow?

A business with robust EBITDA but zero or negative Free Cash Flow is perpetually consuming capital to maintain its operations. To fund expansions or service existing loans, the company must continually rely on external debt or equity dilution. When credit conditions tighten, such companies face acute liquidity crises.
