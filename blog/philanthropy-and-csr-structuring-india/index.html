Section 8 Companies vs Charitable Trusts: Structuring Family Philanthropy & CSR in India

How Indian business families structure philanthropic foundations: Section 8 companies vs public charitable trusts, CSR compliance, 12A/80G approvals, and FCRA.

Short answer

Indian families structure philanthropic foundations using either Public Charitable Trusts (under state public trust acts) or Section 8 Companies (under the Companies Act, 2013). Section 8 companies provide superior corporate governance, pan-India operational mobility, and seamless Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) grant absorption, while charitable trusts offer lower setup complexity but encounter archaic registrar bureaucracy.

Key takeaways

  • Section 8 companies have emerged as the modern institutional standard for Indian family philanthropy, replacing archaic public charitable trusts.
  • Both structures require Section 12AB (income tax exemption) and Section 80G (donor tax deduction) registrations to operate efficiently.
  • Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) mandates under Section 135 require eligible Indian companies to spend 2% of average net profits on qualifying social causes.
  • Receiving international philanthropic donations requires stringent Foreign Contribution Regulation Act (FCRA) licensing from the Ministry of Home Affairs.

Philanthropy among Indian business families has transitioned from ad-hoc religious donations and school sponsorships into institutionalized Family Endowments and Strategic Foundations.

Driven by generational transitions and mandatory Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) regulations under Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013, ultra-high-net-worth families are deploying hundreds of crores of rupees into healthcare, higher education, scientific research, and environmental sustainability.

However, executing high-impact philanthropy requires choosing the right legal entity. Promoters typically evaluate two primary vehicles: the traditional Public Charitable Trust and the modern Section 8 Non-Profit Company.

The Legal Comparison: Trusts vs Section 8 Companies

1. The Public Charitable Trust (The Legacy Model)

Historically, Indian business families formed public trusts governed by state-level statutes (such as the Bombay Public Trusts Act, 1950 in Maharashtra and Gujarat). - The Charity Commissioner Bottleneck: In states with codified trust legislation, every major administrative decision—such as leasing trust land, selling assets, or amending trustees—requires prior written sanction from the local Charity Commissioner. This often ties up philanthropic initiatives in bureaucratic delays spanning years. - State-Level Friction: A trust registered in one state frequently encounters legal challenges when opening operational branches in other Indian states.

2. The Section 8 Company (The Institutional Standard)

Codified under Section 8 of the Companies Act, 2013, this entity is an incorporated limited liability company formed for promoting commerce, art, science, education, charity, or environmental protection, where profits are applied solely toward promoting its objects. - National Recognition: Regulated directly by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA), a Section 8 company operates seamlessly across all Indian states without requiring state-level Charity Commissioner clearances. - Board Governance: Operates with a formal Board of Directors, audited balance sheets, and standardized annual MCA filings, providing institutional credibility for corporate donors and international partners.

Tax Registrations: 12AB, 80G, and the 5-Year Re-registration Rule

Operating an approved philanthropic foundation in India requires three mandatory income tax registrations:

  1. Section 12AB Registration (Tax Exemption on Income): Under Section 11 and 12 of the Income Tax Act, a foundation’s income (donations, investment yield, interest) is completely exempt from income tax, provided at least 85% of income is applied toward charitable objects in India during the financial year.
  2. Section 80G Approval (Donor Tax Deduction): Enables individual and corporate donors to claim a 50% deduction on their donations against their taxable income.
  3. The 5-Year Renewal Mandate: Following recent amendments to the Income Tax Act, charitable registrations are no longer perpetual. All institutions must renew their 12AB and 80G registrations every five years, submitting detailed audit trails proving compliance with statutory charitable definitions.

Absorbing Corporate CSR Capital: Form CSR-1

Under Section 135 of the Companies Act, any Indian corporate with net worth ≥ ₹500 Cr, turnover ≥ ₹1,000 Cr, or net profit ≥ ₹5 Cr must spend 2% of its average net profit over the preceding three years on CSR activities.

Promoters often seek to direct their operating company’s mandatory CSR funds into their own family foundation. This is legally permissible, provided: - The foundation has registered with the MCA by filing Form CSR-1. - The entity has an established track record of at least three years in undertaking similar activities (unless formed directly by the corporate company itself). - The foundation’s programs strictly align with Schedule VII of the Companies Act (e.g., eradicating hunger, promoting education, gender equality, environmental sustainability).

Endowment Investing: Section 11(5) Restrictions

A common pitfall for family offices is attempting to invest the philanthropic foundation’s corpus into venture capital, equities, or high-yield private credit.

Under Section 11(5) of the Indian Income Tax Act, charitable funds are strictly prohibited from investing in equities, private equity, or commercial loans. Foundation capital must be held exclusively in: - Government Securities (G-Secs and State Development Loans). - Post Office Savings Accounts and Scheduled Bank Fixed Deposits. - Units of UTI or mutual funds specifically approved by the Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT). - Debt securities issued by public sector undertakings (PSUs) or public financial institutions.

Investing foundation funds in unapproved commercial securities results in immediate forfeiture of Section 12AB tax-exempt status, exposing the entire accumulated corpus to maximum marginal tax rates.

By establishing a Section 8 Company governed by an independent impact committee, Indian business families transform statutory CSR obligations into durable, institutionalized generational legacies.

Frequently asked questions

What is the primary operational difference between a Section 8 Company and a Public Charitable Trust?

A Section 8 Company is a registered corporate body governed by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) under the Companies Act, 2013, operating with a Board of Directors across India. A Public Charitable Trust is governed by state-specific laws (e.g., Bombay Public Trusts Act, 1950) and requires cumbersome local Charity Commissioner approvals for property disposal.

Can a family foundation absorb mandatory CSR funds from the family's operating business?

Yes, provided the Section 8 company or trust has registered with the MCA by filing Form CSR-1, has an established track record of at least three years in undertaking similar activities, and complies with Rule 4 of the Companies (CSR Policy) Rules, 2014.

How does Section 80G benefit family donors?

Section 80G provides individual and corporate donors with a 50% tax deduction on donations made to approved charitable institutions, subject to a ceiling of 10% of the donor's adjusted gross total income.
