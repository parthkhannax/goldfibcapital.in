Family Office Asset Allocation in India: The Multi-Asset Model

How Indian family offices construct multi-asset portfolios across equities, private credit, unlisted venture, gold, and offshore assets.

Short answer

Indian family offices construct multi-asset portfolios by moving beyond legacy 60/40 equity-debt models. Leading desks allocate 40% to listed Indian equities, 20% to private credit and structured debt yielding 14% to 16%, 15% to venture capital and unlisted growth equity, 15% to global dollar assets via GIFT City or Dubai, and 10% to physical gold and cash reserves.

Key takeaways

  • The traditional 60/40 equity-debt model fails in India due to high inflation, currency depreciation, and low real post-tax debt yields.
  • Private credit has emerged as the premier fixed-income replacement, providing 14% to 16% gross yields with senior secured collateral.
  • Offshore diversification via GIFT City or UAE structures protects purchasing power against secular 3% to 4% annual rupee depreciation.
  • Liquidity tiering across three buckets (Operating Cash, Yield Generation, and Illiquid Growth Alpha) prevents forced selling during market downturns.

The conventional 60/40 balanced portfolio—allocating 60% to listed equities and 40% to government bonds or debt mutual funds—is structurally obsolete for Indian family offices managing ₹100 crore to ₹1,000 crore.

In the Indian macro environment, domestic retail inflation averages 5% to 6%, while real wealth erosion is compounded by a secular 3% to 4% annual depreciation of the Indian Rupee against the US Dollar. Following the removal of long-term debt mutual fund indexation in 2023, traditional debt instruments yield less than 5% net of top-bracket taxes. For an ultra-high-net-worth family, a 60/40 allocation results in negative real post-tax compounding.

Sophisticated Indian family offices have transitioned to an Endowment-Style Multi-Asset Allocation Architecture. By embracing illiquidity premiums, structured private credit, direct equity concentration, and cross-border currency hedges, these offices target a portfolio return of 14% to 16% XIRR while maintaining structural resilience against economic drawdowns.

The Four Building Blocks of Indian Family Wealth

1. Listed Indian Equities (35%–45% Core)

Indian public equities represent the primary engine of long-term real capital growth. Rather than outsourcing the entire allocation to passive mutual funds, family offices deploy a barbell approach: - Core Direct Compounding (60% of equity): A concentrated internal basket of 15 to 20 dominant Indian market leaders with high ROCE (> 20%), low promoter pledge, and durable pricing power. - Satellite Alpha (40% of equity): Deployments into specialized boutique PMS vs AIF managers running small-cap discovery or quantitative factor models.

2. Private Credit and Performing Debt (15%–25% Yield Anchor)

Private debt has permanently replaced fixed deposits and debt mutual funds. Family offices lend directly to mid-market Indian enterprises or invest via SEBI-registered Category 2 AIF credit funds. - Senior Secured Lending: Underwritten with 1.5x to 2.0x collateral cover on hard real estate or operating cash flows. - Yield Expectations: 13.5% to 16.5% gross IRR with monthly or quarterly cash distributions. - Insolvency Protection: Structural protections including escrow accounts, personal promoter guarantees, and pledge of controlling promoter shares enforceable under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC).

3. Unlisted Direct Co-Investments and Venture (10%–20% Illiquidity Alpha)

Rather than acting solely as passive Limited Partners (LPs) in institutional venture funds, Indian family offices demand direct co-investment rights. They invest in Series B through Pre-IPO rounds alongside tier-one venture sponsors, eliminating management fees and carried interest while retaining direct board observation and governance rights.

4. Global Dollar Assets via Offshore Hubs (10%–20% Currency Hedge)

Holding 100% of family assets in Indian Rupees exposes the balance sheet to sovereign concentration risk. Family offices utilize the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) or the Overseas Direct Investment (ODI) framework to deploy dollar liquidity through Dubai vs GIFT City structuring. Capital is deployed into US dollar private credit, global ETFs, and GCC infrastructure yielding stable 7% to 9% USD returns.

Rebalancing Triggers and Liquidity Management

The greatest danger in multi-asset management is becoming illiquid during an economic crisis. Venture commitments and private credit drawdowns are capital calls that must be honored regardless of market conditions.

Family offices maintain strict dynamic rebalancing corridors: 1. Asset Class Bands: Each asset class has a target weight ±5%. If listed equities rally from 40% to 48% of total corpus, profits are systematically trimmed and redirected into liquid yield assets. 2. Cash Call Buffer: Desks maintain a rolling 18-month liquidity buffer covering all unfunded AIF capital commitments, lifestyle expenses, and contingent tax liabilities in sovereign Treasury Bills and arbitrage funds.

The Governance Rhythm: Setting the Investment Policy Statement (IPS)

An asset allocation model is only as effective as the discipline behind it. Leading family offices codify their strategy into a binding Investment Policy Statement (IPS): - Mandates maximum exposure caps (no more than 5% of net worth in a single unlisted company; no more than 10% in a single listed stock). - Defines authorized asset classes and prohibited speculative instruments (e.g., unhedged naked derivative writing). - Establishes a quarterly review rhythm for the Family Investment Committee to audit risk metrics and manager performance against benchmarks.

By organizing capital across distinct liquidity tiers and embracing private credit alongside equity alpha, Indian family offices secure multi-generational wealth against inflation and macro volatility.

Frequently asked questions

What is the typical equity allocation for an Indian family office?

Institutional Indian family offices typically allocate 35% to 50% of their investable corpus to listed equities. This is split between direct high-conviction compounders, factor-driven systematic strategies, and specialist boutique PMS or Category 3 AIF managers.

Why do Indian family offices prefer private credit over traditional debt mutual funds?

Following the removal of indexation benefits on debt mutual funds in the Finance Act 2023, debt mutual funds are taxed at the investor's slab rate. Private credit offers a significant yield premium (14% to 16% IRR vs 7% to 8% in liquid debt), backed by hard collateral and cash-flow escrows.

How much should an Indian family office allocate to offshore assets?

Most mature family offices target 15% to 25% of their net worth in global dollar assets. This is executed using the RBI Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) or Overseas Direct Investment (ODI) route into GIFT City FIFs or UAE holding entities to hedge rupee currency risk.
