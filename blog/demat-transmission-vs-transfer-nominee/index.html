Demat Transmission vs Transfer: Operational Mechanics After Patriarch Decease

The difference between demat share transmission and transfer in India: nominee rights vs legal heirs, succession certificates, probate, and CDSL/NSDL filings.

Short answer

Demat transmission occurs by operation of law upon the death of a shareholding account holder, transferring securities to surviving joint holders or nominees without consideration or stamp duty. In contrast, a demat transfer is an inter-vivos sale or gift between living parties requiring execution slips and potential capital gains tax assessments.

Key takeaways

  • A nominee is merely a statutory trustee and custodian of demat shares, not the ultimate beneficial owner.
  • Ultimate ownership of transmitted shares is governed strictly by the deceased's Will or Indian succession law.
  • Joint demat accounts with 'Either or Survivor' mandates allow instantaneous transmission to the surviving holder upon filing Form Annexure A.
  • For transmissions without nomination or joint holders exceeding ₹5 lakh in market value, a Succession Certificate or Probate is mandatory.

Following the demise of a family patriarch or substantial equity investor, one of the most critical operational tasks facing executors and family offices is settling listed and unlisted securities.

Millions of shares across CDSL and NSDL demat accounts must be legally and operationally transitioned to the next generation.

However, executors frequently confuse Demat Transfer with Demat Transmission.

Making this distinction correctly prevents severe depository rejections, months of bureaucratic delays, and contentious courtroom succession battles between appointed nominees and rightful legal heirs.

Nominee vs Legal Heir: The Critical Legal Distinction

The most pervasive misconception in Indian wealth management is the belief that appointing a nominee on a demat account makes them the legal owner of the shares upon death.

This is legally incorrect:

  1. The Role of the Nominee: The nominee is simply a fiduciary designated by the account holder to facilitate seamless discharge of the shares from the Depository Participant (CDSL/NSDL). The DP transmits the shares into the nominee’s demat account to avoid securities remaining in limbo.
  2. The Rights of the Legal Heir: The beneficial ownership of the shares belongs exclusively to the legal heirs designated in the deceased’s probated Will, or, in the absence of a Will, to heirs determined by personal succession laws (Hindu Succession Act, Indian Succession Act).
  3. The Conflict: If a father appoints his eldest son as the sole nominee on his ₹100 crore demat account, but bequeaths his shares equally to his son and daughter in his registered Will, the son must legally transfer 50% of the shares to his sister. If he refuses, the daughter can successfully sue in civil court to recover her rightful inheritance.

The Operational Mechanics of Transmission

The documentation and complexity of a demat transmission depend on how the original account was structured:

Scenario 1: Joint Demat Account (Fast-Track)

If the deceased held a joint demat account with their spouse or adult child: - The securities do not need to go through probate or succession verification. - The surviving joint holder submits a Transmission Request Form (Annexure A) accompanied by an original or notarized death certificate and Client Master Report (CMR). - The DP deletes the deceased’s name from the account or transmits the holdings into a new demat account in the survivor’s sole name within 7 business days.

Scenario 2: Sole Holder with Registered Nominee

If the deceased was the sole account holder and had registered a valid nomination: - The nominee submits Form ISR-5 (Transmission Request Form) along with KYC documents and the death certificate. - The DP transmits the portfolio into the nominee’s demat account within 15 to 21 days without requiring a succession certificate from court.

Scenario 3: Sole Holder Without Nomination (The Estate Nightmare)

If the deceased failed to register a nominee and the portfolio value exceeds ₹5 lakh (the statutory SEBI threshold): - The DP cannot release the shares based merely on death certificates or family affidavits. - The legal heirs must petition the competent civil court to obtain a Succession Certificate (for movable property) or a Probate of the Will (in Mumbai, Kolkata, and Chennai presidential areas where probate is mandatory for testamentary transfers of immovable/movable assets). - Civil court probate proceedings typically take between 9 and 24 months, during which the entire portfolio remains frozen—unable to sell shares or rebalance holdings during market drawdowns.

Post-Transmission Tax Consequences for Legal Heirs

When shares are transmitted to a legal heir or nominee, the tax rules under the Indian Income-tax Act are exceptionally favorable:

  1. Zero Tax at Transmission: Under Section 47(iii), the transmission of capital assets under a Will or inheritance is not considered a ‘transfer’, meaning zero income tax or capital gains tax is triggered upon receipt.
  2. Cost of Acquisition Steps In: Under Section 49(1), when the legal heir eventually sells the transmitted shares, their cost of acquisition is deemed to be the original purchase price paid by the deceased.
  3. Period of Holding Includes Deceased’s Tenure: Under Section 2(42A), the holding period of the deceased is combined with the holding period of the heir to determine whether the eventual capital gain is short-term or long-term.

Frequently asked questions

What is the legal difference between a nominee and a legal heir regarding demat shares?

As reaffirmed by the Supreme Court of India in multiple landmark rulings, a nominee appointed on a demat account acts solely as a statutory caretaker or trustee to receive the securities from the Depository Participant (DP). The nominee holds the shares on behalf of the legal heirs entitled to the estate under the deceased's Will or applicable personal succession laws.

What documentation is required to transmit shares from a joint demat account upon death of the primary holder?

In a joint demat account, the surviving joint holder submits: (1) a formal transmission request form (Annexure A), (2) an original or notarized death certificate, (3) self-attested PAN and Aadhaar of the surviving holder, and (4) a new Client Master Report (CMR) of the surviving holder's individual demat account.

Is stamp duty or capital gains tax payable on the transmission of demat shares upon death?

No. Under Section 47(iii) of the Income-tax Act, any transfer of a capital asset under a gift, will, or irrevocable trust/inheritance is expressly excluded from the definition of transfer, meaning zero capital gains tax is payable. Furthermore, transmission by operation of law attracts zero depository stamp duty.
