When an investment banker pitches an upcoming IPO on national television, the conversation invariably revolves around **'The Issue Size'**: *'Company XYZ is raising ₹5,000 crore from the public!'*.

To the untrained retail investor, that headline implies that the company is receiving ₹5,000 crore to build automated factories, acquire competitors, hire top talent, and supercharge corporate compounding.

In reality, more than half of all capital raised in Indian IPOs over the past decade never touched the operating company's bank balance.

The single most fundamental fork in equity capital markets is the division between a **Fresh Issue** and an **Offer for Sale (OFS)**. Understanding who gets the money—and what the promoters are signaling through their personal share sales—is the first line of defense in buy-side research.

## The Structural Mechanics: Fresh Issue vs OFS

To see the economic divergence clearly, compare the balance-sheet mechanics of both structures:

```viz
type: flow
title: "Capital flow: Fresh Issue vs Offer for Sale (OFS)"
steps:
  - title: "Fresh Issue Route"
    sub: "Company issues new shares -> Public pays cash -> Cash lands on corporate balance sheet -> Equity capital expands."
    tag: "Balance Sheet Cash"
  - title: "Productive Deployment"
    sub: "Management deploys cash into machinery, solar capex, or debt retirement -> Increases future operating earnings."
    tag: "Growth Fuel"
  - title: "Offer for Sale (OFS) Route"
    sub: "Promoter/PE sells existing shares -> Public pays cash -> Cash transfers directly to seller bank accounts."
    tag: "Personal Liquidity"
  - title: "Corporate Balance Sheet Impact"
    sub: "Zero rupees enter the company. Outstanding share count is unchanged; only ownership registry shifts."
    tag: "Zero Capex Added"
caption: "Fresh issues build long-term corporate book value; Offer for Sale is an insider liquidity event."
```

In a **Fresh Issue**:
- New equity shares are minted, diluting the percentage holdings of existing shareholders.
- The net proceeds directly increase the company's cash reserves or decrease long-term bank borrowings.
- Book value per share and tangible net worth expand, strengthening credit ratings and lowering borrowing costs.

In an **Offer for Sale (OFS)**:
- No new shares are created; existing shares simply transfer from one demat account to another.
- The company's balance sheet does not receive a single rupee. Net debt remains unchanged.
- The selling shareholders pay securities transaction tax and capital gains tax, depositing the remaining proceeds in their personal wealth portfolios.

Examining real-world examples illuminates the promoter psychology behind each bucket:

### 1. The Pure Sponsor Cashout (100% OFS)
As detailed in our analysis of [Hyundai Motor India's ₹27,870 crore IPO](/blog/hyundai-motor-india-ipo-valuation-analysis/), the South Korean parent company monetized a 17.5% stake. Because the Indian subsidiary was already cash-rich, not a single rupee of fresh equity was required.

While financially legitimate for mature multinational subsidiaries, a 100% OFS means public shareholders take on all equity market risk while providing risk-free liquidity to the parent entity.

### 2. The Balanced Transition (Fresh + OFS)
In listings like [Swiggy](/blog/swiggy-ipo-valuation-unit-economics-vs-zomato/) (₹4,499 Cr Fresh, ₹6,828 Cr OFS) or [Afcons Infrastructure](/blog/afcons-infrastructure-ipo-order-book-valuation/) (₹1,250 Cr Fresh, ₹4,180 Cr OFS), the offering serves two masters: it injects growth capital into the operating entity while providing an exit window for decadal private equity funds and debt-laden promoters.

### 3. The Pure Deleveraging Engine (100% Fresh Issue)
In utility and infrastructure listings like [NTPC Green Energy](/blog/ntpc-green-energy-ipo-renewable-valuation/), the state-backed sponsor sold zero existing shares. The entire ₹10,000 crore was structured as fresh equity deployed into debt retirement and solar array construction, immediately reducing corporate interest expenses.

## The Promoter Signal: Why OFS Proportions Matter

In fundamental equity research, capital allocation is an unfiltered window into management's honest assessment of future business prospects.

Ask the obvious question: **If a company's prospects are so luminous that public investors are urged to pay 60x earnings, why are the founders and private equity backers selling tens of millions of shares today?**

Statistical data across five years of Indian public listings reveals that IPOs with high Fresh Issue proportions systematically outperform heavy OFS listings over a 3-year holding period.

The reasons are operational:
- **Balance Sheet Strength**: Fresh capital pays down expensive working capital debt, unlocking an immediate boost to Profit Before Tax (PBT) through lower finance costs.
- **Capacity Creation**: Fresh capital builds plants, acquires automated machinery, and funds technology R&D, generating incremental revenue 24 months post-listing.
- **Incentive Alignment**: In a heavy OFS, promoters and early venture investors have already locked in generational wealth. Their hunger to aggressively defend market share against younger competitors frequently diminishes.

::: warn The '100% Private Equity Exit' Red Flag
Be exceptionally cautious when a private equity fund sells 100% of its holding in an IPO, leaving zero residual skin in the game. Private equity firms possess insider information, board seats, and detailed monthly MIS reports. When they choose complete liquidation over partial de-risking, it strongly implies they believe the company's compounding rate has peaked.
:::

## The Dilution Math: Why Fresh Issues Require Scrutiny

While Fresh Issues fund business expansion, they introduce an important mathematical consequence: **Equity Dilution**.

When a company issues millions of new shares:
- The total share capital expands.
- Future net profit must be divided across a larger number of shares, reducing **Earnings Per Share (EPS)** unless the newly deployed capital generates a Return on Equity (RoE) higher than the cost of capital.

For example, if a company with 10 crore outstanding shares earning ₹50 crore PAT (EPS = ₹5.00) issues 5 crore new shares in an IPO, the post-issue share count becomes 15 crore shares. 

To maintain that same ₹5.00 EPS, the company's net profit must immediately jump by 50% to ₹75 crore. If the new factory takes two years to become operational, reported EPS will suffer a severe near-term drag.

## Institutional Takeaway: Follow the Money Trail

When evaluating an upcoming initial public offering, ignore the marketing roadshow video and turn directly to page 60 of the prospectus: the **'Objects of the Issue'** table.

- If the issue is dominated by fresh capital dedicated to measurable debt repayment and tangible capacity expansion, the offering is structured to compound enterprise value.
- If the issue is a 100% Offer for Sale designed to facilitate private equity exits at peak multiple valuations, demand a substantial valuation discount before deploying capital.

For family offices and institutional allocators, true due diligence is not about being first to subscribe; it is about ensuring that your capital is financing the future growth of the business, rather than funding the exit of the previous owners.
